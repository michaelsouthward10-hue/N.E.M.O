GREEK MYTHOLOGY · ATTICA

Athena and
the olive tree.

A contest for a city, a salt-water spring, and a tree that became a lasting emblem of Athens.

A Mythos retelling · 4 minute read

When the gods sought cities where they would receive their own honours, Poseidon and Athena both laid claim to Attica. In the account preserved by Apollodorus, Poseidon struck the Acropolis with his trident and brought forth a sea called Erechtheis.

Athena came after him. With Cecrops, the early king of Attica, as witness, she planted an olive tree in the precinct of Pandrosus. The two gods disputed possession of the land, so the Twelve Gods were appointed as judges. They awarded Attica to Athena because Cecrops testified that she had planted her tree first.

The goddess named the city Athens after herself. Poseidon, angered by the verdict, flooded the Thriasian plain, Apollodorus adds. The story links a divine contest to the identity of a city—and gives the olive a place in its founding memory.

One account, not the only one.

Apollodorus names the Twelve Gods as judges. Other ancient summaries describe the contest differently. Pausanias, writing as a traveller centuries later, records sculptures of Athena displaying an olive plant and Poseidon displaying a wave. Our retelling follows Apollodorus and keeps those accounts distinct.
